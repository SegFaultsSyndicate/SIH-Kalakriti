# Kalakriti motif grammar

Six traditions license the entire visual system. Every asset in every batch
must trace back to exactly one of these (or, for the handloom grid, to the
product's own subject matter). This document is the contract: a second
illustrator should be able to produce a new, consistent asset from this page
alone, without seeing the rest of the system.

---

## Warli

**Structural logic.** Warli reduces the human and animal figure to three
primitives — circle (head), two triangles apex-to-apex (torso and pelvis),
and straight lines (limbs) — arranged for narrative action rather than
anatomical accuracy. The economy is the point: nothing is drawn that motion
or meaning doesn't require.

**Geometric primitives.** Circle, triangle (apex-to-apex pair), straight
line segments at the joints. No curves outside the head circle.

**Licensed for.** Empty-state illustrations and onboarding scenes that show
a person doing something (weaving, carrying, selling) — anywhere the product
needs a human figure without depicting a specific person.

**Misuse.** Reproducing an existing Warli painting's composition (a
specific ritual scene, a specific artist's panel layout). Adding facial
features, clothing detail, or colour fill to the figures — Warli figures are
line-and-shape, not costume studies. Using Warli figures decoratively behind
text with no illustrative purpose.

---

## Jaali

**Structural logic.** Jaali is pierced stone or wood lattice: a repeating
unit cell (usually built on a 4-, 6-, or 8-fold symmetry) where the negative
space — what's cut away — carries the pattern, not the material that
remains. Structural integrity constrains the design: no cut can be so large
it would collapse the physical lattice, which is why jaali patterns read as
dense but never fragile.

**Geometric primitives.** A repeating unit cell on a square or hexagonal
grid; interlocking straight lines and arcs; negative-space apertures sized
consistently within one pattern.

**Licensed for.** Decorative page backgrounds at low opacity (patterns
batch), the QR code surround (provenance page), dividers between page
sections.

**Misuse.** Filling every aperture solid (that's not jaali, that's a filled
grid — the negative space is the subject). Scaling one pattern's unit cell
so large it reads as a standalone motif rather than a repeating field.
Placing a jaali pattern at high opacity behind body text.

---

## Block print (Ajrakh, Bagru, Dabu)

**Structural logic.** Block-printed textiles build a field from a small set
of hand-carved blocks stamped in registration, producing repeating bordered
motifs with resist-dye negative space: the white or undyed areas are
deliberate, not gaps. Ajrakh in particular is built in layered colour
passes (resist, then dye, repeated), which is why its motifs read as dense
but ordered rather than busy.

**Geometric primitives.** A bordered repeat unit (usually a rosette or
star-like center motif with a running border pattern framing it);
resist-negative space held as a consistent line-weight within the motif.

**Licensed for.** Card surface variants (block-print-edged cards),
provenance page ornament, section dividers that need more visual weight
than a jaali pattern.

**Misuse.** Reproducing an identifiable Ajrakh, Bagru, or Dabu print in
full (a real historical block design) rather than an abstracted
bordered-repeat structure. Mixing an Ajrakh-style centre motif with a
Bagru-style border in one asset without noting both traditions in the
asset's comment.

## Kolam / Rangoli

**Structural logic.** Kolam is drawn as a single continuous line (or a small
number of continuous lines) woven around a grid of dots, with the dots never
directly touched by the line — the line loops around each dot. The
continuity of the line, and its relationship to the dot grid, is the whole
form.

**Geometric primitives.** A dot grid (square or diagonal); one continuous
looping line that encircles but never crosses a dot; rotational or mirror
symmetry around the grid's centre.

**Licensed for.** Loading and progress indicators where a continuous line
metaphor fits (the "thread through four stations" motif in Batch 7 borrows
this continuity logic, though it is documented separately as
handloom-derived); decorative underlines and dividers that want a drawn,
continuous feel rather than a straight rule; and L-shaped corner ornaments
framing featured content (Batch 3), where the dot grid runs along two edges
and the line closes around it.

**Misuse.** A kolam-derived line that crosses itself in a way the tradition
wouldn't produce, or that touches/cuts through a dot. Using kolam as a
generic "Indian pattern" texture disconnected from its dot-grid logic.

## Charkha, lotus, mandala

**Structural logic.** Used sparingly and abstractly, per Batch 0. The
charkha (spinning wheel) reduces to a wheel-and-spindle silhouette; the
lotus to a radially symmetric petal cluster; the mandala to concentric
rings of repeating unit motifs. All three are historically loaded (charkha
with the independence movement, lotus and mandala with religious
iconography) and are used here only as pure geometry — wheel-and-spokes,
radial petals, concentric rings — never as a symbol standing in for its
historical or religious meaning.

**Geometric primitives.** Charkha: circle + radiating spokes + a secondary
smaller circle (spindle) offset from centre. Lotus: radially arrayed
petal shapes (an even count, typically 6–8), each a simple pointed-oval,
never a deity's attribute. Mandala: concentric rings, each ring a
repeating unit at consistent angular spacing.

**Licensed for.** Charkha: the primary loading spinner (its literal
mechanical function maps directly to a spinner). Lotus and mandala: rare,
small ornamental accents (e.g., the seal) — never as a page-filling
pattern, never as the default motif when another tradition would fit.

**Misuse.** Any figurative deity, hand gesture (mudra), or other religious
attribute attached to the lotus or mandala shape. Using the charkha as
decoration unrelated to spinning, thread, or loading/progress. Treating
any of these three as the "default Indian motif" — they are the most
loaded of the six and are used least.

## Handloom structure (warp and weft)

**Structural logic.** This one is ours, not adapted from an external
tradition: the product catalogs handmade textiles, and the loom's own
structure — vertical warp threads held under tension, horizontal weft
threads passed through them — is a native grid motif for a textile
marketplace. Unlike the other five, there is no living-community
attribution concern here beyond "handloom weaving in general," so it can be
used more freely, including as the base grid for skeleton shimmer and
loading states.

**Geometric primitives.** A regular grid of vertical (warp) and horizontal
(weft) lines at a consistent pitch; a "thread passing through" animation
reads as a weft line traveling across a fixed warp.

**Licensed for.** Loading skeleton shimmer, the ML pipeline progress
indicator ("thread passing through four stations"), sync-state icons
("thread being drawn" / "completed stitch"), background texture behind
buyer-facing content.

**Misuse.** None specific to cultural attribution — but keep it visually
distinct from the jaali lattice (jaali is apertures in a solid field;
handloom grid is thread lines with open space between, no solid field to
speak of) so the two don't collapse into "generic grid pattern" in a
teammate's eyes.

---

## Cross-tradition rule

No single asset may combine primitives from two of the five external
traditions (Warli, jaali, block print, kolam, charkha/lotus/mandala) unless
the asset's comment explicitly names both and explains why — the default is
one tradition per asset. The handloom grid may combine with any other
tradition as a background/foreground pairing (e.g., a Warli figure over a
handloom-grid texture) since it functions as ground rather than motif.
